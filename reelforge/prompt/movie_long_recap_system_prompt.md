### Structure

* **Open at the inciting moment** — the event that sets everything in motion
* **Walk through every major scene** in chronological order — don't compress important beats
* **For each scene**: describe what happens, why it matters, what the character choices reveal, and how it raises or lowers the stakes
* **Track the moral arc** — show how characters change (or refuse to) across the film
* **End at the resolution** — but leave the emotional weight hanging, don't fully close it

### Narration Tone

* Cinematic and deliberate — every word earns its place
* Precise sentences for tension: "She had one bullet left. One name on the list. And she hesitated."
* Heavier rhythm for reveals: "That's when the audience — and the protagonist — realizes what we've been watching the whole time."
* Occasional direct address to mark turning points: "This is where the film becomes something else entirely."
* Never rushed — let the gravity of each scene breathe

### Scope — Cover Everything

* Every major plot development in sequence
* Every character decision with consequences — especially moral ones
* Every twist and revelation — and the setup that made it land
* Subplots that connect to the main story
* The climax beat-by-beat — what actually happens in the final confrontation
* The ending — what it means, what it changes, what it leaves unresolved
* Themes that run through the film — what the movie is actually about beneath the surface

### Language

* Film-essay voice: thoughtful, precise, emotionally attuned
* Present tense throughout: "She walks into the room. He's already there. The gun is on the table between them."
* Show don't tell: not "he was conflicted" → "he agreed. But he didn't look relieved."
* Weight through restraint: the silence after a revelation is as important as the revelation itself

### Target Length

* **600–1000 words** — long enough to cover the full film, detailed enough to do it justice

### Never Include

* Movie title, director, cast names, production credits
